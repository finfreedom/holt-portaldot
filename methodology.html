Methodology

How a score is computed, and how to catch us if it isn’t

Holt ranks validators and allocates stake between them. Both steps are arithmetic over public chain data, and both are published here in enough detail to be reproduced without asking us anything.

The last section tells you what we cannot claim yet. It is there because a methodology page that only lists strengths is marketing.

What goes into a score

Nine factors were designed. On the network as it stands, four of them separate validators and the rest return the same answer for everyone. Those are switched off rather than carried as decoration.

A factor with no spread contributes zero, not “the same to everyone”. Values are normalised against the observed range; when that range is empty the factor switches itself off and the remaining weights are renormalised. The interface says “does not separate” beside it, which is more honest than performing analysis where there is none — and it explains why the score rests on four columns rather than nine.

The gate, and why it kills the displayed score too

A validator charging 100% commission is excluded: a nominator delegating to it receives exactly nothing. The gate suppresses the score shown in the table as well, not just participation in the allocation.

Without that, such a validator scores well on self-stake — often 100% of its own stake — and lands above the largest validator in the table. A person reads that ordering as “a respectable option”. Gated validators are listed separately with the reason stated.

Offences are not slashes. staking.unappliedSlashes is empty and the slash fraction on every record is zero. All 414 records are unresponsiveness signals from ImOnline. Calling them slashes in an interface would be a lie, so we don’t.

Scoring and allocation are kept apart

Scoring answers one question: is this validator sound? It knows nothing about concentration. A large validator taking 26% commission and producing blocks reliably is a perfectly good choice for an individual nominator, and docking its score for other people’s delegations would be dishonest.

Allocation answers a different question — where should this stake go? — and that is where a per-validator cap applies. You see both: that the validator is sound, and why it is not given everything.

What a nominator submits is a list of targets and nothing else. Phragmén decides the proportions. So the published figure is what the election returns, not what we would like it to return, and the drift between intent and outcome is shown after the fact from erasStakers.

Determinism

Two runs over the same era must produce the same bytes, on any machine, or the attestation below is worthless.

  • Inputs are the chain and the config. Nothing else. No wall clock, no randomness, no network state beyond the finalised snapshot of that era.
  • Integer arithmetic throughout. Values are basis points — u16, 0 to 10 000 — never floating point. Division truncates, and the truncation is part of the specification.
  • Canonical ordering. Validators sort by raw public key ascending; factors by their fixed id.
  • The config version is part of the result. Change a weight and the root changes.
value_bp = (raw − min) × 10000 // (max − min)      inverted where lower is better
         = inactive                                  when max == min

score_bp = Σ(weight_i × value_bp_i) // Σ(weight_i)   over active factors only

The attestation, byte for byte

Hash is blake2-256 everywhere. Every number is big-endian. Each hash is domain-separated so that a leaf can never be passed off as a node.

leaf_i = blake2_256(
    "holt.v1.validator"
  ‖ account_id        32 bytes, raw public key
  ‖ score_bp          u16
  ‖ gated             u8, 1 if excluded by the gate
  ‖ factors_hash      32 bytes
)

factors_hash = blake2_256(
    "holt.v1.factors"
  ‖ for each factor, in ascending factor_id:
    factor_id  u8
  ‖ value_bp   u16
  ‖ weight_bp  u16, zero if the factor is off
  ‖ active     u8
)

leaf_0 = blake2_256("holt.v1.config" ‖ canonical_config_bytes)

node   = blake2_256("holt.v1.node" ‖ left ‖ right)

Factor ids are permanent and never reused:

1 commission   2 self-stake   3 offences   4 identity
5 liveness     6 consistency  7 oversubscription

The tree is binary. Leaves in canonical order: leaf_0 first, then validators by ascending public key. An odd node duplicates the last. What goes on chain is one call:

commit(era: u32, root: [u8;32], engine_version: u32)

Owner-only, fixed at instantiation. There is no second way to write a root.

Check us

Nothing here requires our cooperation. Take the era snapshot from the public chain, take our published config, recompute the leaves and the root by the description above, and compare it with what sits in the contract. A mismatch means we showed something other than what we committed.

That dual implementation exists for one reason: a single implementation proves only that the code is self-consistent. Two, written to a written spec, test whether the spec is complete.

What we cannot claim yet

Stated plainly, because this is the section a sceptical reader checks first.

  • The pool is not proven on V3. Portaldot’s own developer guide does not cover staking at all, the V3 testnet runs one validator and zero pools, and nobody has demonstrated that NominationPools works on their build. Our first act there is one transaction that creates a pool and nominates — before any interface is built on top of it.
  • No live nomination on mainnet. Holt holds no authority over anyone’s stake, by design and by choice. It reads, scores, and publishes. The allocation figure is a computed outcome of the election, verified on era 3863, not a position we are running.
  • Correlated-failure clusters were looked for and not found. That was the original thesis. Grouping by payout address, stash/controller, identity.superOf and coincident gaps turned up nothing. An earlier run appeared to find clusters; it had grouped by payout variant rather than address. The finding was withdrawn.
  • Four factors, not nine. Five return the same answer for every validator on this network. They stay in the code and switch on when the network gives them something to measure.
  • Era history is not infinite. The staking pallet keeps 84 eras. Ask it about era 3780 today and it answers zero. The data survives in archive-node state — on an RPC that happens to retain it — and in our own copy. Neither was promised to anyone.
